Adversarial Systems & Complex Adaptive Dynamics Research

dis·sen·sus /dɪˈsɛnsəs/ — from Latin dissentire, to disagree. In political philosophy (Rancière, Mouffe), the productive disagreement that remains when consensus is impossible or undesirable. The group is named for it: not dysfunction, function.

We study complex adaptive systems — markets, institutions, minds, machine collectives — and specifically what happens in them when the parts want different things. Systems like these rarely settle. They persist by continually absorbing the friction of their own disagreement, and that process, rather than any equilibrium, is what we try to measure.

01 · What we do

Complexity science, applied to systems that disagree with themselves

Dissensus is an independent research group in London. The subject is complex adaptive systems: many interacting agents, no central controller, behaviour at the aggregate level that none of the parts intends. That description fits a cryptocurrency market, a regulator, a multi-agent AI system and an institution deciding who gets a vote, which is why the group's papers look scattered until you notice they are asking one question in four different places.

The specific question is what happens when the parts want different things. Coordination has overhead; delegating anything to anyone produces friction; and that friction is measurable in terms of how well interests align, how much is at stake, and how badly intent degrades in transmission. Systems of this kind do not converge to a tidy equilibrium and then stop. They persist by continually working off the cost of their own internal disagreement.

It is one programme rather than a collection of side interests, and the work runs from formal proof through simulation to empirical estimation, because a claim of this shape has to survive all three. What is finished, stalled or abandoned is on Research; what is open is on Projects.

03 · Contribute

Three ways into the lab

Dissensus is small, independent, and open at the edges. The research runs regardless, but it runs faster and argues better with more people in the room.

I
Take one task
Every open project is published with the thing that is actually blocking it, sized honestly: a weekend of blind-coding, one inference run, an afternoon of literature sweep, fifteen minutes of arXiv endorsement. The task list is the fastest way in, and none of it requires a CV.
II
Take a project
Six projects are open to collaborators, each listed with what exists, what is missing, and who would move it. Co-authorship is the default where the contribution is substantive, and author order is agreed in writing before the work starts. The records are here, gaps included.
III
Fund the programme
Money buys research time and nothing else: compute, data access, publication costs. What it funds stays public — archived datasets, machine-checked Lean formalisations, and the index running daily. Funding never buys editorial reach. How that works, or back it directly.

Three of the tasks open right now, at the size they actually are:

A weekend, ×2 coders Blind-code the focusing-event table with the outcome column stripped Public-policy or regulatory-studies researcher who works with focusing-event or agenda-setting theory Fifteen minutes Endorse an arXiv submission in econ.GN or cs.CY Anyone already holding endorsement rights in econ.GN or cs.CY Two to three weeks Design a codebook, train multiple raters, and report a real reliability statistic on a stratified subset of the 215 responses Measurement or psychometrics researcher who has run annotation properly

The lab started this year and very little about it is settled, which is the actual reason to join now rather than later: what it works on and how it decides a result is finished are still open questions. There is no funding yet, so none of these roles are paid — the limits are set out plainly rather than buried.
